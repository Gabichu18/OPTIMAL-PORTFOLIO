How short selling and leverage work in this model

Short selling. You borrow shares and sell them: you gain if the price falls and lose if it rises, with no limit on the loss. The cash raised pays for extra long positions, so 130/30 means 130% of your capital long and 30% short. You stay 100% net invested but can bet against the assets the model likes least. You pay a borrow fee on the value shorted, and you owe the dividends, which the total returns used here already include.

Leverage. You borrow at the risk-free rate plus your broker's spread to hold more than 100% of your capital. Expected return, volatility and drawdowns all scale with leverage, but the Sharpe ratio does not improve: the borrowing spread lowers it slightly. Leverage is for reaching a risk level, not for better risk-adjusted returns.

Why the frontier turns into a straight line. With borrowing allowed, the cheapest way to take more risk is to lever one portfolio: the one with the best Sharpe ratio measured against your borrowing rate (the best to lever diamond on the Frontier tab), not to pile into riskier assets. Below that point no borrowing is needed; above it the frontier is that portfolio at 1× to your maximum leverage, then riskier portfolios at maximum leverage.

What a weight means. With shorts or leverage on, weights are shares of your capital and are no longer rescaled to 100%. Negative means short. Whatever does not add up to 100% net is cash earning the risk-free rate, or money borrowed if you are above 100%. The max weight per asset applies to longs and shorts, measured on the invested portfolio before leverage: at 2× a 15% limit allows 30% of your capital.

Losses beyond your capital. Backtests, stress tests and the Monte Carlo rebalance to the same exposure every month and charge interest and fees monthly. A month that loses more than your equity wipes the portfolio out. In reality the broker would sell you out earlier, at the margin call shown above (estimated for all positions falling together). Retail margin accounts under US Reg T allow about 2× overnight; EU retail CFDs on single stocks are capped at 5×.

What stays long-only. Max Sortino, min downside, risk parity, resampled, equal and cap-weighted remain long-only reference portfolios. Use the leverage used slider to lever any of them. The rolling strategies in the walk-forward backtest are long-only too. Max Sharpe, min variance and the target sliders use your full strategy.

Bootstrap replays real months from the estimation window in random 6-month blocks, keeping crashes, fat tails and co-movement; each asset's average is shifted to its forward expected return. Alpha generated

Realized alpha is the intercept of a regression of each portfolio's monthly return above the T-bill on the S&P 500's return above the T-bill (Jensen, 1968), using today's weights over the estimation window. It is the return the market exposure does not explain. The t-statistic says whether it is distinguishable from luck: above 2 is significant at 95%. The chart compounds that unexplained return month by month: a rising line is alpha being earned, a falling one is alpha being lost. Same caveats as the backtests: the weights and the 43 assets were chosen with hindsight.

Expected alpha (CAPM & SML)

Betas against SPY (S&P 500 with dividends), monthly, over each asset's history in the window. SML: rf + β·ERP. Dots above the line carry positive Jensen's alpha on the expected returns in use.

      Black-Litterman starts from equilibrium returns (CAPM: rf + β × ERP), which carry no opinion, then tilts them toward views in proportion to your confidence. The slider adds each asset's own history as a view; your views below come on top. Exclude 2008–09 crisis (button at the top) removes December 2007 – June 2009 from everything the model estimates: expected returns, risk, correlations, the optimal portfolios and the Monte Carlo. Backtests, stress tests and realized alpha always use the full history, since they report what actually happened. Ledoit-Wolf picks the shrinkage that minimises expected estimation error, computed on the full window. Assets that listed after the window starts are put on the same footing as the rest (Stambaugh, 1997): their history is adjusted for how much better or worse the market did while they traded (Window-adj.), and before listing they are filled with their beta to the S&P 500 plus their own residuals, so volatility and correlations include the dot-com crash and 2008.
