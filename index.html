Bootstrap replays real months from the estimation window in random 6-month blocks, keeping crashes, fat tails and co-movement; each asset's average is shifted to its forward expected return. Downside deviation counts only months below your minimum acceptable return (MAR), currently a year. Alpha generated

Realized alpha is the intercept of a regression of each portfolio's monthly return above the T-bill on the S&P 500's return above the T-bill (Jensen, 1968), using today's weights over the estimation window. It is the return the market exposure does not explain. The t-statistic says whether it is distinguishable from luck: above 2 is significant at 95%. The chart compounds that unexplained return month by month: a rising line is alpha being earned, a falling one is alpha being lost. Same caveats as the backtests: the weights and the 39 assets were chosen with hindsight.

      Black-Litterman starts from equilibrium returns (CAPM: rf + β × ERP), which carry no opinion, then tilts them toward views in proportion to your confidence. The slider adds each asset's own history as a view; your views below come on top. Exclude 2008–09 crisis (button at the top) removes December 2007 – June 2009 from everything the model estimates: expected returns, risk, correlations, the optimal portfolios and the Monte Carlo. Backtests, stress tests and realized alpha always use the full history, since they report what actually happened. Ledoit-Wolf picks the shrinkage that minimises expected estimation error, computed on the full window. Assets that listed after the window starts are put on the same footing as the rest (Stambaugh, 1997): their history is adjusted for how much better or worse the market did while they traded (Window-adj.), and before listing they are filled with their beta to the S&P 500 plus their own residuals, so volatility and correlations include the dot-com crash and 2008.
